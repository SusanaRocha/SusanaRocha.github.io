<p align= "justify">
Cancer remains challenging to treat due to its complexity and heterogeneity, driving continued interest in advanced nanoscale platforms for targeted delivery and diagnostics. DNA origami is a highly programmable nanotechnology in which a long single-stranded DNA scaffold is folded into well-defined nanostructures using short “staple” strands. These nanostructures can be engineered with nanometre precision, enabling control over size, shape, surface chemistry, and labeling—features that make DNA origami an excellent model system to study how nanoscale materials behave in biological environments. <br>
A key open question for the translation of DNA origami-based systems is their biological fate: how efficiently they enter cells, where they traffic intracellularly, how long they persist, how they are degraded, and how these processes differ between 2D monolayer cultures and more physiologically relevant 3D cancer cell spheroids. In this master’s project, the student will design, assemble, and fluorescently label DNA origami nanostructures and systematically investigate their fate in cancer cell models. The project will focus on quantifying uptake kinetics, penetration in spheroids, intracellular localization (e.g., endosomal/lysosomal trafficking), structural stability over time, and potential clearance/degradation pathways.
Depending on progress, the student can explore how parameters such as origami shape, surface charge, coating/functionalization, or targeting ligands influence cellular uptake, spheroid penetration, and intracellular processing.<br>
